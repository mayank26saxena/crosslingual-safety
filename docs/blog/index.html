Why chat models refuse less in Hindi

Asked the same harmful requests in English and in Hindi, two open chat models refuse far less often in Hindi. We look at what they do instead, what changes inside them, and how far steering can bring the refusals back.

Chat models are trained to decline harmful requests mostly in English. It is well known that this safeguard weakens in other languages (Yong et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024). What has been less clear is what the model does instead, and why its behaviour changes when only the language changes. We looked at two popular open 8B models in Hindi, one of the most widely spoken languages in the world. Every hypothesis test below was pre-registered, and failures are reported alongside successes.

01How we measured it

Every harmful request was asked twice, once in Hindi and once in English. Most were written natively in Hindi by human annotators (Aya Red-teaming; Aakanksha et al. 2024) and come with a human English translation. Comparing the same request in both languages rules out the obvious confound: that the Hindi requests are simply different, or harder to understand. We screened every item:

  • Is it actually a request?
  • Is the translation faithful?
  • Does the model understand the Hindi version? We had it restate the request in English. Items it misunderstood show the same gap, so misunderstanding is not the cause.

The held-out set is 404 harmful and 169 harmless request pairs, identical for both models.

Instead of “refused or not”, every reply gets one of four labels. We check them in the order below, so a reply that answers the request counts as complied even if it adds a warning.

LabelWhat the reply doesIn practice
CompliedGives what was asked.Answers the request, even if it adds a warning.
Explicit refusalSays it will not help.“I can’t help with that.”
Soft refusalNeither helps nor says no.Lectures, warns, or answers a safer question instead.
BrokenNot a usable reply.Incoherent, stuck repeating itself, or in the wrong language.

An AI judge assigned the labels. Multilingual AI judges are known to be uneven across languages (Fu & Liu 2025), so we validated ours against a fluent Hindi speaker. Agreement was good on explicit refusal, which the headline numbers rest on (κ = 0.79, even graded blind), and weaker on the boundary between a soft refusal and a reply that warns and then answers. The details are in Appendix A.

03What replaces the refusal?

Explicit refusal drops sharply in both models. Where does it go?

Qwen mostly keeps declining in Hindi, but softly: it warns, lectures or changes the subject, without the clear “I can’t help with that” it gives in English. Counting broken replies as unrefused too, Llama leaves Hindi harmful requests unrefused 1.4–1.9 times as often as Qwen does on the same requests.

04Where do Hindi requests land inside the model?

Interpretability research has found that chat models carry a refusal direction: a single direction in their internal activations that tracks whether a request should be refused (Arditi et al. 2024). We ask where Hindi prompts land on it.

04.1Finding the direction

We fit the direction on English prompts only: it is the difference between the model’s internal state on harmful and on harmless requests, one direction per layer. To pick a layer, we remove (ablate) the direction at each layer and see how much refusal disappears. It peaks at layer 14 in Qwen and layer 10 in Llama, so those are the layers we use.

04.2Where Hindi prompts land

Next we project each prompt onto the direction. A higher score means the model’s internal state points more toward refusing.

04.3What this tells us

This fits earlier results that refusal directions are shared across languages but weaker in lower-resource ones (Wang et al. 2025; Aziz et al. 2026). What is new here is seeing both patterns side by side, in two models, on identical requests.

05Can a targeted push make the models refuse more in Hindi?

We turned each diagnosis into a repair. The aim is simple: more explicit refusal of harmful Hindi requests, without making the model refuse harmless ones or break its replies.

05.1A direction for refusal style

Qwen’s problem looked like wording, so we searched for a second direction: one that separates prompts the model will refuse explicitly from prompts it will refuse softly, fitted on English replies only. It exists. Cross-validated, it predicts explicit versus soft with an AUROC of 0.93, and Hindi prompts sit toward its soft end.

It is also causal. On 150 requests Qwen refused explicitly in English but not in Hindi, adding this English direction to the model’s activations raised Hindi explicit refusal from 10% to 53% and halved compliance, with no change on harmless requests. Prior work shows that safety behaviour is concentrated in the first few tokens of a reply (Qi et al. 2024), which is where an explicit “no” lives. As far as we know, no one had isolated refusal style as its own direction before.

05.2Gated steering

The repair is gated steering, which builds on activation steering (Turner et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024) and its conditional form (Lee et al. 2025):

  1. A gate reads the refusal signal for each prompt.
  2. If the prompt looks harmful, we add a fixed direction to the model’s activations while it writes the reply.
  3. We chose each setting on development requests using rules written down in advance, then tested it once on the held-out requests.

The two repairs differ. Qwen: push toward explicit refusal. Llama: push along the refusal direction itself, with the gate’s threshold set on Hindi prompts. With an English threshold, the gate opened on only 1% of Llama’s Hindi harmful prompts.

05.3How hard to push

We tried several push strengths on development requests and kept the strongest one that stayed inside guards fixed in advance.

05.4What the repairs achieve

The goal is more explicit refusal in Hindi, and both repairs deliver: +17.1 points in Qwen and +14.6 in Llama on held-out requests. We also track English. A push that simply made the model refuse more of everything would raise Hindi refusal too, without fixing anything specific to Hindi, and the gap between the languages shows how much of the gain is Hindi-specific. English refusal also rose, so Hindi caught up by only 1.2 points in Qwen and 7.4 in Llama. Figure 7 shows why English rises too: the gate asks whether a prompt looks harmful, and English harmful prompts score at least as high as Hindi ones. For Llama, a Hindi-set threshold still works. It is the median Hindi development score, so it lets through about half of Hindi harmful requests by design. What is not by design is the other side: the same threshold lets through none of the 169 Hindi harmless requests, because Llama still orders Hindi harmful prompts above harmless ones, only on a smaller scale. Llama’s gap narrows more partly because its English refusal was already near the ceiling (89%), leaving little room to rise.

06Related work

The multilingual gap is well documented for Indic languages too (IndicSafe; IndicSafeEval; SurakshaEval). Refusal directions are largely shared across languages but weaker for low-resource harm, and a gate calibrated on the target language helps Llama in particular (Aziz et al. 2026), which our Llama result independently supports. Conditional steering is established (Lee et al. 2025). What we add:

  • a response-side view: what replaces the refusal;
  • a direction for refusal style;
  • a comparison of two models on identical items;
  • pre-registered repairs, including their failures.

07Limits, and what’s next

What didn’t hold up

A held-out language. We kept a Bengali test set sealed until the end. Qwen’s repair raised Bengali explicit refusal by 24 points, but it also garbled 8% more replies, beyond the limit we had set, so the registered verdict is fail. A follow-up on fresh Bengali requests didn’t reproduce the damage, which weakens but doesn’t overturn that verdict.

A “Hindi switch”. We searched for MLP neurons that carry the English–Hindi difference. 100 neurons (of 307,200) account for three quarters of it, while random neurons account for none. But shifting them made Qwen refuse more in English (+15 points), not Hindi (+1).

Limits

  • Two 8B models, with greedy decoding only.
  • One AI judge and one human grader.
  • No comparison yet against simpler fixes, such as a safety system prompt or translating to English first, which can rival steering (Wu et al. 2025).
  • The projection result hasn’t been tested on harmless requests that merely sound harmful, where cross-lingual separation can collapse (Balani & Panda 2026).
  • The per-layer curves for Llama come from an exploratory run added after the registered studies.

What’s next

  • Compare against those simpler baselines.
  • Build a gate that is selective for Hindi.
  • Run a fresh confirmation set for Llama.

Appendix A: can we trust the labels?

Everything above depends on telling a soft refusal from a reply that warns and then answers. That boundary is genuinely hard. Our human check on Llama (60 replies, graded blind by a fluent Hindi reader) fell below the agreement bar we had registered (κ = 0.64, bar 0.7). Most disagreements were exactly that case. On review, the grader re-applied the rubric strictly (a warning followed by an answer counts as complying), and agreement rose to 0.89. We report both numbers.

The headline numbers rest on explicit refusal, which was reliable even blind (κ = 0.79). Qwen’s judge had passed its check with κ = 0.82.

Appendix B: how we tried to keep ourselves honest

  • Rules first. Before every experiment we committed what we would measure, how we would pick a setting and what would count as success; later changes went into dated addenda. One analysis, the per-layer Llama curves in Figure 2, was added afterwards for this post. It is descriptive and tests no hypothesis.
  • Tune once, test once. Test gains came in at about 80% of the best development gains, as expected from picking the best of several settings.
  • No harmful text. Requests are summarised, never quoted. The only reply text shown is the redacted excerpts in sections 02 and 05, with anything usable for harm blacked out.
