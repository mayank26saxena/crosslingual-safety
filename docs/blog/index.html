Same gap, different signatures: why two chat models refuse less in Hindi

We asked two open models the same harmful requests in English and in Hindi. Both refuse far less in Hindi, but they fail in different ways, inside and out, and a repair only reaches Hindi in Llama once its trigger is calibrated on Hindi.

TL;DR

On the same 404 harmful requests, explicit refusal falls from 50% to 19% in Qwen3-8B and from 89% to 47% in Llama-3.1-8B when the request is in Hindi rather than English.

The models fail differently. Qwen hedges: its lost refusals mostly become warnings and redirections that never say no. Llama hedges or complies, in similar shares.

Inside, both models still rank Hindi harmful requests above harmless ones on the English "refusal direction", but the gap between the two shrinks to about a third of the English gap. The models differ in which side moves. Qwen's Hindi harmful prompts score at full English strength, while its harmless Hindi prompts rise toward them. Llama's Hindi harmful prompts score at only 28% of the English value.

Repairs that push the model's activations raise Hindi refusal by 15–17 points, but also lift English, so neither closes the gap. Every test was pre-registered, and we report what failed; the one exploratory analysis is labelled as such.

Chat models are trained to decline harmful requests mostly in English. It is well known that this safeguard weakens in other languages. What has been less clear is what the model does instead, and why its behaviour changes when only the language changes. We looked at two popular open 8B models in Hindi, one of the most widely spoken languages in the world.

How we measured it

Every harmful request was asked twice, once in Hindi and once in English. Most of them were written natively in Hindi by human annotators (Aya Red-teaming) and come with a human English translation. Comparing the same request in both languages rules out the obvious confound: that the Hindi requests are simply different, or harder to understand. We screened every item:

  • Is it actually a request?
  • Is the translation faithful?
  • Does the model understand the Hindi version? We had it restate the request in English, and kept the comprehension check honest: items the model misunderstood show the same gap.

The final held-out set is 404 harmful and 169 harmless request pairs, identical for both models.

Instead of "refused or not", every reply got one of four labels, assigned in order:

  • complied: gave what was asked, even with a warning attached;
  • explicit refusal: says it will not help;
  • soft refusal: neither helps nor says no; it lectures, warns or answers a safer question;
  • broken: incoherent or in the wrong language.

An AI judge assigned the labels. We validated it against a fluent Hindi speaker before trusting it (more on that below).

Experiment 1

What replaces the refusal?

Explicit refusal drops sharply in both models. The interesting part is where it goes.

The two models land in different places. Qwen mostly keeps declining in Hindi, but softly: it warns, lectures or changes the subject, without the clear "I can't help with that" it gives in English. Llama loses more refusals overall, and a large share of them become answers. Counting broken replies as unrefused too, Llama leaves Hindi harmful requests unrefused 1.4–1.9 times as often as Qwen does on the same requests.

Experiment 2

Where do Hindi requests land inside the model?

Interpretability research has found that chat models carry a refusal direction: a single direction in their internal activations that tracks whether a request should be refused (Arditi et al. 2024). We found this direction using English prompts only, then measured where Hindi prompts land on it, for the same requests.

Put Figures 2 and 3 together and the two models look alike on one measure and different on another. In both, the Hindi gap between harmful and harmless requests is about a third of the English gap ( in Qwen, in Llama), and in both the order survives. What differs is which side moved. In Qwen, all of the shrinking comes from the harmless side: Hindi harmful prompts sit exactly where English ones do, but harmless Hindi prompts sit far higher than harmless English ones. In Llama, most of it comes from the harmful side: harmful Hindi prompts fall to 28% of the English value, while harmless Hindi prompts rise only a little.

That points the two models at different problems. In Qwen the refusal signal for Hindi harm is as strong as in English, so its weaker refusals probably arise later, in how the refusal is worded. In Llama the signal is still well ordered but small, so most Hindi harmful prompts score well below where English harmful prompts sit. This fits results showing that refusal directions are shared across languages but weaker in lower-resource ones (Wang et al. 2025; Aziz et al. 2026). What is new here is the two patterns side by side, in two models, on identical requests.

Experiment 3

Is "how" a model refuses written somewhere inside it?

Qwen's problem looked like wording, so we searched for a second direction: one that separates prompts the model will refuse explicitly from prompts it will refuse softly, fitted on English replies only. It exists. Cross-validated, it predicts explicit versus soft with an AUROC of 0.93, and Hindi prompts sit toward its soft end.

It is also causal. On 150 requests Qwen refused explicitly in English but not in Hindi, adding this English direction to the model's activations raised Hindi explicit refusal from 10% to 53% and halved compliance, with no change on harmless requests. As far as we know, no one had isolated refusal style as its own direction before.

Experiment 4

Can a targeted push close the gap?

We turned each diagnosis into a repair with gated steering:

  1. A gate reads the refusal signal for each prompt.
  2. If the prompt looks harmful, we add a fixed direction to the model's activations while it writes the reply.
  3. We chose each setting on development requests using rules written down in advance, then tested it once on the held-out requests.

The two repairs:

  • Qwen: push toward explicit refusal.
  • Llama: push along the refusal direction itself, with the gate's threshold set on Hindi prompts. With an English threshold, the gate opened on only 1% of Llama's Hindi harmful prompts.

How hard to push?

A stronger push raises refusal more, but at some point the model starts producing garbled text. We tried several strengths on development requests and kept the strongest one that stayed inside the guards we had fixed in advance.

Both repairs work in the sense that Hindi refusal goes up. Neither is language-selective. The gate asks "does this look harmful?", and English harmful prompts trigger it at least as often as Hindi ones.

Figure 7 also shows why a Hindi-set threshold could work for Llama. The threshold is the median Hindi development score, so it lets through about half of Hindi harmful requests by design. What is not by design is the other side: because Llama still orders Hindi harmful prompts above harmless ones, only on a smaller scale, the same threshold lets through none of the 169 Hindi harmless requests. The cost is that English harmful prompts sit higher still, so the gate opens on nearly all of them. Llama narrows the gap anyway, partly because its English refusal was already near the ceiling (89%), leaving little room to rise.

What didn't work

A held-out language. We kept a Bengali test set sealed until the end. Qwen's repair raised Bengali explicit refusal by 24 points, but it also garbled 8% more replies, beyond the limit we had set, so the registered verdict is fail. A follow-up on fresh Bengali requests didn't reproduce the damage, which weakens but doesn't overturn that verdict.

A "Hindi switch". We searched for MLP neurons that carry the English–Hindi difference. 100 neurons (of 307,200) account for three quarters of it, while random neurons account for none. But shifting them made Qwen refuse more in English (+15 points), not Hindi (+1).

Can we trust the labels?

Everything above depends on telling a soft refusal from a reply that warns and then answers. That boundary is genuinely hard. Our human check on Llama (60 replies, graded blind by a fluent Hindi reader) fell below the agreement bar we had registered (κ = 0.64, bar 0.7). Most disagreements were exactly that case. On review, the grader re-applied the rubric strictly (a warning followed by an answer counts as complying), and agreement rose to 0.89. We report both numbers.

The headline numbers rest on explicit refusal, which was reliable even blind (κ = 0.79). Qwen's judge had passed its check with κ = 0.82.

How we tried to keep ourselves honest

  • Rules first. Before every experiment we committed what we'd measure, which settings we'd try, how we'd pick one, and what would count as success. Changes went into dated addenda: one bug, one infrastructure crash, and the label review above. One analysis was added afterwards, for this post: the per-layer Llama curves in Figure 2. It is descriptive and tests no hypothesis.
  • Tune once, test once. Test numbers came in at about 80% of the best development numbers, as you'd expect from picking the best of several settings.
  • No harmful text. No harmful request or reply appears in this post or the repository; everything is cited by item id.

Related work

The multilingual safety gap is well documented (Yong et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024), including for Indic languages (IndicSafe; IndicSafeEval). Refusal is mediated by a direction in activation space (Arditi et al. 2024) that is largely shared across languages (Wang et al. 2025). Low-resource harm projects more weakly onto it, and a gate calibrated on the target language helps Llama in particular (Aziz et al. 2026), which our Llama result independently supports. Conditional steering is established (Lee et al. 2025).

What we add:

  • a response-side view: what replaces the refusal;
  • a direction for refusal style;
  • a comparison of two models on identical items;
  • pre-registered repairs, including their failures.

Limits, and what's next

Limits:

  • two 8B models with greedy decoding;
  • one AI judge and one human grader;
  • no comparison yet against simpler fixes, such as a safety system prompt or translating to English first;
  • the projection result hasn't been tested on harmless requests that merely sound harmful;
  • the per-layer curves for Llama come from an exploratory run added after the registered studies.

Next: those baselines, a language-selective gate, and a fresh confirmation set for Llama.
