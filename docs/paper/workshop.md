# Same Gap, Different Signatures: Diagnosing and Repairing the Hindi Refusal Gap in Two Open Chat Models

## Abstract

Open chat models refuse harmful requests less reliably outside English. Most evaluations measure this with per-language refusal or
attack-success rates, which hide two questions: what replaces the missing refusal, and why the model's behaviour changes when only the
language does. We measure the gap on *paired* requests, the same request asked in English and in Hindi, screened for being a real request,
for translation fidelity and for comprehension. We grade every reply with a four-way label (comply, explicit refusal, soft refusal, broken),
using an LLM judge validated against a fluent human.

**The gap.** On 404 held-out pairs, explicit refusal falls from 51% to 19% in Qwen3-8B and from 89% to 47% in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

**What replaces it.** Qwen's lost refusals are mostly soft: it warns or redirects without declining. Llama's split between soft refusal
and compliance.

**The internal signatures differ too.** On a refusal direction fitted in English, Qwen's Hindi harmful prompts project at English
strength, while Llama's reach 28% of it. In both models the direction still ranks Hindi harmful above harmless prompts, with the Hindi
gap about a third of the English one; Qwen's harmless side rises, while Llama's harmful side falls. A separate direction that predicts explicit versus soft refusal places Hindi prompts toward its
soft end. Adding that direction turns soft refusals into explicit ones.

**Two repairs, each tested once on held-out items:**

- *Qwen:* a gated push along the explicit-versus-soft direction raises Hindi explicit refusal by 17 points. English rises by 16, so the
  gap barely moves, and the repair failed a sealed Bengali confirmation on garbled output.
- *Llama:* a push along the refusal direction, with a gate threshold set on Hindi data, raises Hindi explicit refusal by 15 points and
  narrows the gap by 7. It narrowly misses its pre-registered bars.

Every hypothesis test was pre-registered, and the failures are reported; one descriptive same-item analysis was added afterwards and is
labelled exploratory.

## 1 Introduction

Refusal is the main behavioural safeguard of open chat models, and it is trained mostly on English data. Since 2023 a series of papers has
shown that harmful requests in other languages are refused less often. The early work used translated attacks and benchmarks (Yong et al.
2023; Deng et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024). More recent work turns to Indic languages specifically (Pattnayak & Chowdhuri 2026; Banerjee et
al. 2026; Mondal et al. 2026). In parallel, interpretability work has shown that refusal is mediated by a direction in activation space
(Arditi et al. 2024). That direction is roughly shared across languages (Wang et al. 2025), and lower-resource languages project more
weakly onto it (Aziz et al. 2026).

Two questions remain open. **First, what replaces the missing refusal?** Prior work scores refusal as a binary or as a harm score, so a
model that hedges in Hindi looks the same as one that complies. **Second, does the same behavioural gap have the same internal cause in
different models?** Most mechanistic studies argue for one account: either harm is recognised but the refusal fails, or the harm signal
itself is weak. They rarely compare models on identical items. On identical items we find a third pattern: in both models the Hindi
harmful–harmless order survives on the refusal direction but the separation shrinks, and the models differ in which side moves.

We study both questions in Hindi for Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Hindi is officially supported by both model families. We use
default, non-adversarial requests and the same items for both models.

**Contributions.**

1. **A response-side decomposition of the gap.** We use paired, screened, same-request measurement with a four-way label validated
   against a fluent human. In Qwen the lost explicit refusals become mostly soft refusals. In Llama they split between soft refusal and
   compliance, and Llama leaves Hindi harmful requests unrefused more often (§4.1, Fig. 1).
2. **An explicit-versus-soft refusal direction.** To our knowledge, this is the first activation direction for refusal *style* rather
   than refusal itself. Hindi prompts sit toward its soft end, and adding it converts soft refusals into explicit ones (§4.2).
3. **A contrast of internal signatures on identical items.** Qwen's Hindi harmful prompts project on the English refusal direction at
   English strength; Llama's reach 28% of it. Both keep the Hindi harmful–harmless order with about a third of the English separation,
   so the models differ in which side moves, not in how much separation survives (§4.2, Fig. 2).
4. **Pre-registered repairs, with their failures.** A repair can pass every registered check without narrowing the gap, and can fail
   confirmation in a held-out language. A diagnosis-matched repair narrows the gap more, but still acts on English (§4.3, Fig. 3).

## 2 Related work

**Multilingual safety gaps.**
- *Early measurements.* Yong et al. (2023) showed that translating attacks into low-resource languages bypasses GPT-4's safeguards.
  MultiJail (Deng et al. 2024) and XSafety (Wang et al. 2024) measured unsafe outputs across many languages; XSafety includes Hindi and
  Bengali. These studies use translated English prompts and per-language rates, mostly on closed models.
- *Indic benchmarks.* IndicSafe (Pattnayak & Chowdhuri 2026), SurakshaEval (Banerjee et al. 2026) and IndicSafeEval (Mondal et al. 2026)
  extend this to Indic languages. IndicSafeEval evaluates our two models in Hindi, but uses machine-translated persuasion attacks and
  reports attack-success rates.
- *Closest methodological cousin.* Huang et al. (2026) decompose attack success by response language. We decompose by refusal style.

**Refusal directions.**
- *Origin.* Arditi et al. (2024) showed that a single difference-of-means direction mediates refusal in many open chat models.
- *Refinements.* Wollschläger et al. (2025) show refusal can occupy multi-dimensional cones. Zhao et al. (2025) show that harmfulness and
  refusal are encoded separately, at the last instruction token and the last template token respectively. We read the latter, so we
  describe *refusal*-direction projections, not harm recognition.
- *Across languages.* Wang et al. (2025) find refusal directions approximately parallel across 14 languages (not including Hindi), with
  weaker harmful–harmless separation outside English, especially in Llama-3.1-8B. Aziz et al. (2026) find that low-resource harm projects
  lower but stays separable, and call these "action failures, not representation failures". Kompella & Mahajan (2026) find
  language-invariant harm detection but late, separate refusal writing in an Indic mixture-of-experts model. Our two models show both
  patterns on the same items.
- *Style.* Joad et al. (2026) find distinct refusal directions for different *reasons* for refusing. To our knowledge, none for refusal
  *style*.

**Conditional steering.**
- *Gated steering.* Activation steering adds a fixed vector during generation (Turner et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024). Conditional
  Activation Steering (Lee et al. 2025) gates it on a detector.
- *Calibration across languages.* Aziz et al. (2026) gate on a classifier calibrated with target-language examples, and report that Llama
  needs this calibration while Qwen and Gemma barely do. Our English-set gate for Qwen and Hindi-set gate for Llama independently agree.
- *Reliability.* Steering vectors generalise unevenly (Tan et al. 2024; Braun et al. 2025), and prompting can beat steering (Wu et al.
  2025). We therefore test once on held-out items and report a garbled-output guard.

**Refusal taxonomies and judges.** Prior schemes distinguish full from partial refusal (Röttger et al. 2024) or name indirect refusals
(Cui et al. 2024). LLM judges are less reliable outside English (Hada et al. 2024; Fu & Liu 2025). We judge in the original script and
validate against a fluent human on each model.

## 3 Setup

### 3.1 Data

**Sources.**
- *Harmful requests:* Aya Red-teaming (Aakanksha et al. 2024), written natively in Hindi (Devanagari) by paid annotators with human
  English translations, plus the human-translated Indic set IndicSafe.
- *Harmless controls:* PolyGuard (Kumar et al. 2025) and IndicSafe.
- *Capability:* Global-MMLU (Singh et al. 2025).

**Screening.** Candidates were screened in order:
1. *Request type:* is it a request, and is it harmful? An LLM screener, validated against a fluent human after adjudication (92%
   agreement, an upper estimate).
2. *Translation fidelity.*
3. *Comprehension:* the model restates the Hindi request in English, and a judge compares the restatement with the English version.

Items the model misunderstood show the same gap as items it understood. The final held-out set is 404 harmful and 169 harmless pairs,
identical for both models. Bengali MultiJail stayed sealed until the confirmation test.

### 3.2 Generation and grading

**Generation.**
- Greedy decoding, system prompt "You are a helpful assistant."
- Up to 1,024 new tokens in Hindi and Bengali, 512 in English.
- Fixed, length-sorted batches. The arms of each comparison share identical batches.

**Grading.** Each reply gets the first label that fits:
- **COMPLY:** provides what was asked, even partly or with warnings.
- **REFUSE_EXPLICIT:** says it will not or cannot help.
- **REFUSE_SOFT:** shows it understood, but neither provides nor declines.
- **BROKEN:** no usable response.

Replies from every arm are shuffled under opaque ids before grading.

**Judge validation.**
- *Qwen:* the judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5) was chosen against one fluent Hindi reader on 150 replies. κ is 0.82 for refused versus not, and
  0.91 for explicit refusal versus the rest.
- *Llama:* a pre-registered 60-reply check fell below its bar when graded blind (four-label κ 0.643 [0.48, 0.79], bar 0.7). Most
  disagreements were replies that warn and then answer. After the grader re-applied the rubric to the disagreements, agreement was 0.893
  (an upper estimate). We use the reviewed labels as the human reference. Explicit refusal, our primary outcome, was the most reliable
  label even blind (κ 0.785).

### 3.3 Directions and steering

Activations are read at the last template token after the user turn.

**Refusal direction.**
- Difference of means between English harmful and harmless prompts (391 and 190).
- Layer chosen by directional ablation against a first-token refusal score.
- Valid if ablation removes at least 50% of the held-out harmful–harmless score gap.

**Explicit–soft direction.**
- Difference of means between English prompts whose replies were explicit refusals and those that were soft refusals.
- Layer chosen by five-fold cross-validated AUROC; valid at 0.75 or above.

**Steering.**
- A gate opens when the refusal-direction score exceeds a threshold τ: the median score of development harmful prompts.
- While the gate is open, a fixed vector is added at a later layer, at every position.
- Settings were chosen on development items by a frozen rule: the largest Hindi explicit gain, subject to guards on harmless over-refusal,
  broken output, English refusal and MMLU. The chosen setting is then tested once on held-out items.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 The gap and what replaces the refusal

![Figure 1](figures/fig1_what_replaces_refusal.png)

**Figure 1.** Replies to the same 404 held-out harmful requests in English and Hindi.

| 404 harmful pairs | Qwen3-8B | Llama-3.1-8B |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit refusal, English → Hindi | 51% → 19% | 89% → 47% |
| Gap (95% CI) | −32.2 [−37.4, −26.5] | −42.3 [−47.3, −37.4] |
| Lost refusals become mostly… | soft refusal | soft refusal and compliance, in similar shares |
| Hindi unrefused (comply + broken) | 24.0% | 33.2% |

In Qwen, the move is from explicit to soft refusal. On a 448-pair superset of the test split, explicit refusals fall from 230 to 82 and
soft refusals rise from 170 to 265. In Llama, the lost refusals split between compliance and soft refusal (88 and 75 of 172; p = 0.35). On
the same items, Llama leaves Hindi harmful requests unrefused more often than Qwen: +9.2 points [+4.5, +14.1] counting broken replies, and
+13.9 [+9.4, +18.3] counting compliance alone. The gap holds on both data sources, and on items the model demonstrably understood.

### 4.2 Internal signatures

![Figure 2](figures/fig2_projection.png)

**Figure 2.** Mean projection on each model's English-fitted refusal direction for the same harmless and harmful test pairs, in
English (ring) and Hindi (dot), with bootstrap 95% intervals. Hindi harmful prompts reach 100% of the English value in Qwen and 28% in
Llama; harmless Hindi prompts in Qwen shift toward refusal.

**Refusal direction.**
- *Qwen.* The direction is valid at layer 14: ablation removes 64% of the held-out score gap. Hindi harmful prompts project at English
  strength (paired shift +0.17 [+0.02, +0.30]). Harmless Hindi prompts also sit high, which lowers harmful–harmless separation (tier A, 1,174 pairs: AUROC
  0.88 against 0.96).
- *Llama.* The direction is valid at layer 10 (ablation removes 94%). Hindi harmful prompts reach only 28% of the English mean.
- *Same items, both models (exploratory).* On the 404 + 169 test pairs, the direction still ranks Hindi harmful above harmless prompts
  (AUROC 0.92 in Qwen, 0.97 in Llama; 0.98 and 0.99 in English), and the Hindi gap is about a third of the English gap in both (0.33 and
  0.30). All of Qwen's shrinkage comes from harmless Hindi prompts rising; about three quarters of Llama's comes from harmful Hindi
  prompts falling. Llama's Hindi signal is weak in scale but well ordered. The Llama numbers come from a forward-pass run added after the
  registered studies; its layer-10 values reproduce the registered projections.

**Explicit–soft direction.**
- *Qwen.* Valid at layer 24 (CV AUROC 0.93; split-half cosine 0.96). It transfers to Hindi without refitting (AUROC 0.76), and Hindi
  prompts sit toward the soft end (paired shift −10.9 [−12.1, −9.6]).
- *Causal check in Qwen.* On 150 pairs refused explicitly in English but not in Hindi, adding the English vector raises Hindi explicit
  refusal from 10% to 53% (+42.7 [+34.0, +51.3]), halves compliance, and leaves harmless prompts unchanged.
- *Llama.* The direction is valid at layer 18 (AUROC 0.925) with the same shift, but it was fitted on only 11 English soft refusals.

### 4.3 Repairs

![Figure 3](figures/fig3_repairs.png)

**Figure 3.** Explicit refusal before and after each repair, on held-out pairs, run once.

| Repair | Hindi explicit | English explicit | Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Qwen:** gate on refusal score (English-set τ), push along the explicit–soft direction at layer 25 | +17.1 [+13.1, +21.0] | +15.8 | 31.9 → 30.7 | passes all registered checks; gap barely narrows |
| **Qwen**, sealed Bengali confirmation (148 items) | +23.6 [+16.2, +31.1] | +6.8 | 23.6 → 6.8 | **fails**: BROKEN +8.1 [+2.0, +14.9], limit 5 |
| **Llama:** gate with Hindi-set τ, push along the refusal direction at layer 11 | +14.6 [+11.1, +18.3] | +7.2 | 42.3 → 34.9 | **misses** bars (+15; gap 10) |

**Selection on development data.**
- *Qwen:* nine gate and strength settings were tried; the best gave +21.4 on development, and the test gave 80% of that.
- *Llama:* five pushes were tried, R3 was chosen, and the test gave 81% of its development gain (+18.0).

**Guards.** No repair raised harmless refusal or lowered Global-MMLU. Both guards are weak, because the gates rarely opened on benign
inputs. A later forced-gate MMLU run for Qwen found no loss.

**Two diagnostic results.**
- *Gate calibration matters for Llama.* With the English-set threshold, the gate opened on 1% of Llama's Hindi harmful prompts. With the
  Hindi-set threshold it opened on 47%. With the Hindi threshold, even the explicit–soft push qualified on development data (+16.7).
- *No Hindi-only lever.* Attribution patching found 100 MLP neurons (of 307,200) that recover 75% of Qwen's internal English–Hindi
  difference; random sets recover none. Shifting them raised English explicit refusal (+15.2) but not Hindi (+1.2).

## 5 Discussion

**Response-side labels matter.** A binary refusal rate would have reported the same kind of gap in both models. The four-way label shows
that Qwen hedges and Llama hedges or complies. Read together with the projections, it suggests different internal routes to the same
behavioural gap: a similarly shrunken Hindi separation, reached from opposite sides (harmless Hindi rising in Qwen, harmful Hindi falling
in Llama).

**Gates are not language-selective.** Both repairs gate on how harmful-looking a prompt is, and the gates opened as often (Qwen) or more
often (Llama) on English (Fig. 4). Llama's Hindi-set threshold (the Hindi development median) opens on about half of Hindi harmful
prompts by construction; that it opens on none of the 169 Hindi harmless prompts reflects the preserved order. It also opens on 92% of
English harmful prompts. Raising Hindi refusal is therefore much easier than closing the gap. Gap-narrowing should be a registered outcome
of any cross-lingual repair; Qwen's repair met every check we registered and still left the gap.

**The hard label boundary.** A reply that warns and then answers is the hardest case for both human and judge. It drove the blind
disagreement on Llama, so we report blind and adjudicated agreement together.

**Figure 4.** Gate scores of every held-out prompt (unsteered) at the gate layer, with each model's threshold as run and the share of each
group above it. Qwen: layer 14, English-set threshold. Llama: layer 10, Hindi-set threshold.

## 6 Limitations

- **Models and decoding:** two 8B open models, one revision each, greedy decoding only.
- **Labels:** one LLM judge and one human grader, so inter-human agreement is not measured. Bengali and Spanish labels have no human
  check. The Llama human check fell below its bar blind, and the adjudicated agreement is an upper estimate.
- **Projection claims:** not tested against hard negatives (harmless requests that sound harmful). Cross-lingual probe transfer can
  collapse on such items (Balani & Panda 2026). No separate harmfulness direction was fitted. The same-item AUROC and gap-ratio
  comparison is exploratory.
- **Baselines:** no prompting or translate-to-English baseline, which prior work suggests can rival steering (Wu et al. 2025).
- **Data:** the harmful and harmless sets come from different sources, and much of the harmful set is from one dataset. Many Hindi
  replies reach the token limit.
- **Re-use and confirmation:** the Llama repair was tested on its diagnosis items (no Llama setting was chosen on them). Every Bengali
  confirmation item is now used.

## Ethics statement

We release no harmful request or reply text. Results cite items by id, and harmful datasets are fetched from their upstream sources.
Steering vectors that raise refusal could, with the sign flipped, lower it; the directions we use follow published methods (Arditi et al.
2024), and we release no new jailbreak.
