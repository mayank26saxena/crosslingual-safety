# Mechanistic and interpretability accounts of cross-lingual safety failure, and how multilingual LLMs represent language

Scope: 2023 to 2026 (search date 2026-11-15). Each entry gives the citation, what the paper did and found, and how it relates to our study. Our study: in Qwen3-8B, Hindi harmful prompts reach English-strength projection on an English-fitted refusal direction, but the refusals are worded softly. In Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Hindi harm projects weakly on that direction and the lost refusals become compliance. Separately, we found 100 Hindi-associated MLP neurons, located by attribution patching toward English activations, that recover most of the internal English-Hindi difference. Shifting them changes behaviour in English, not Hindi.

Verification legend: **[V-arXiv]** means title, authors, date and abstract were checked against the arXiv API in this session. **[V-web]** means the venue or detail was checked on a proceedings or official page in this session. **[UNVERIFIED]** means the detail comes from memory or from a secondary summary and must be checked before citing.

## Q1. Language-specific neurons and features: LAPE, Kojima et al., and others. Do they apply to safety?

### Takeaway
A small set of language-specific neurons or SAE features (under about 1 to 5 percent), concentrated in the first and last layers, controls input and output language identity. Middle layers are largely shared. Prior work reports that intervening on these neurons steers output language, but does not improve low-resource task ability (Mondal et al. 2025). The only safety-neuron paper with a multilingual claim (SN-Tune, ICLR 2025) reports safety neurons that are language-specific. The 2026 papers instead report a small cross-lingually *shared* safety-neuron subset. Our negative result (Hindi-associated neurons move English behaviour, not Hindi behaviour) fits the Mondal et al. finding. It also fits the view that language neurons carry language identity rather than the safety computation.

### Cited Findings
- **Tang, Luo, Huang, Zhang, Wang, Zhao, Wei, Wen (2024). "Language-Specific Neurons: The Key to Multilingual Capabilities in Large Language Models." ACL 2024. arXiv:2402.16438.** [V-arXiv; venue from arXiv comment] Proposes LAPE (language activation probability entropy). In LLaMA-2, BLOOM and Mistral, proficiency in a language rests on a small subset of neurons, mostly in the top and bottom layers. Deactivating or activating them steers the output language. *Relation:* this is the standard neuron-finding method, but it selects neurons by activation statistics. We selected neurons causally, with attribution patching toward English activations, and the target was safety behaviour rather than language identity. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16438)
- **Kojima, Okimura, Iwasawa, Yanaka, Matsuo (2024). "On the Multilingual Ability of Decoder-based Pre-trained Language Models: Finding and Controlling Language-Specific Neurons." NAACL 2024. arXiv:2404.02431.** [V-arXiv] Across six languages (no Hindi), language-specific neurons overlap by less than 5 percent and sit in the first and last few layers. Tampering with under 1 percent of neurons changes the probability of target-language output. *Relation:* this supports reading our 100 neurons as partly language-identity units. It also predicts that shifting them alters which language is produced more than whether the model refuses. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02431)
- **Zhao, Zhang, Chen, Kawaguchi, Bing (2024). "How do Large Language Models Handle Multilingualism?" NeurIPS 2024. arXiv:2402.18815.** [V-arXiv; NeurIPS venue UNVERIFIED in session, but I am confident of it] Proposes the MWork pipeline (understand, then "think" in English in the middle layers, then generate in the target language) and PLND (parallel language-specific neuron detection). Deactivating language-specific neurons hurts that language, and fine-tuning them with little data improves it. *Relation:* MWork predicts that a safety decision made in the English-like middle layers should transfer. Our Qwen result (English-strength harm recognition) fits this. Llama's weak projection does not, which suggests the "convert to English" step is incomplete for Hindi in Llama. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18815)
- **Mondal, Sen, Singhania, Jyothi (2025). "Language-specific Neurons Do Not Facilitate Cross-Lingual Transfer." NAACL 2025 Workshop on Insights from Negative Results (oral). arXiv:2503.17456.** [V-arXiv] Neuron-specific interventions and LoRA on Llama 3.1 and Mistral Nemo, with neurons found by LAPE or activation-probability thresholds, do not improve low-resource XNLI or XQuAD performance. *Relation:* this is the closest precedent for our negative neuron result, and it uses one of our models, Llama 3.1. Our finding extends theirs from task ability to safety, and adds that the neurons have an English-side effect. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17456)
- **Gurgurov, Trinley, Al Ghussin, Baeumel, van Genabith, Ostermann (2025). "Language Arithmetics: Towards Systematic Language Neuron Identification and Manipulation." AACL 2025. arXiv:2507.22608.** [V-arXiv] Applies LAPE to Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-Nemo and Aya-Expanse across 21 languages. Neurons cluster in deeper layers, non-Latin scripts are more specialised, and related languages share neurons. Steering by activation addition or multiplication works better for high-resource languages, and the models show "fallback" language selection. *Relation:* this covers the same Llama model. The non-Latin specialisation (Devanagari) suggests Hindi neurons are script- and identity-heavy, which fits our observation that they move English behaviour rather than Hindi safety. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22608)
- **Wang, Haddow, Wu, Peng, Birch (2024). "Sharing Matters: Analysing Neurons Across Languages and Tasks in LLMs." arXiv:2406.09265.** [V-arXiv; venue UNVERIFIED, possibly a 2025 *ACL venue] Classifies neurons as all-shared, partial-shared, specific or non-activated across nine languages. All-shared neurons matter most for correct output. *Relation:* this suggests the safety-relevant computation lives in shared neurons, so a "Hindi-specific" safety lever is a priori unlikely. That matches our result. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09265)
- **Deng, Wan, Zhang, Yang, Feng (2025). "Unveiling Language-Specific Features in Large Language Models via Sparse Autoencoders." ACL 2025 (main). arXiv:2505.05111.** [V-arXiv] Introduces a monolinguality metric for SAE features. Ablating language-specific features damages only that language, and the features can steer generation language. *Relation:* this is the SAE analogue of LAPE. Running a feature-level version of our neuron search would be a natural extension. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05111)
- **Zhao, Zhang, Xie, Goyal, Kawaguchi, Shieh (2025). "Understanding and Enhancing Safety Mechanisms of LLMs via Safety-Specific Neuron." ICLR 2025.** [V-web: ICLR proceedings page] Reports safety neurons under 1 percent of parameters, mostly in self-attention in the first several layers, described as "language-specific". SN-Tune tunes only these neurons and cuts Llama3-8B-Instruct's harmful score from 65.5 to 2.0. A secondary summary says safety-neuron overlap across English, Chinese and Thai is under 30 percent [UNVERIFIED, secondary]. The arXiv id was not checked. *Relation:* this paper claims language-specific safety neurons exist and can be tuned, which is the hypothesis our neuron study tested for Hindi. Our result (the neurons act on English) is evidence against a clean Hindi-specific safety locus in our setting. — [ICLR 2025 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/6d2666e2cf44088cc57204fbc5ef7f34-Abstract-Conference.html)
- **Chen, Wang, Yao, Bai, Hou, Li (2024; NeurIPS 2025). "Towards Understanding Safety Alignment: A Mechanistic Perspective from Safety Neurons." arXiv:2406.14144.** [V-arXiv; venue from arXiv comment] Uses inference-time activation contrasting to find about 5 percent safety neurons. Patching only those neurons restores over 90 percent of safety performance. The setting is English only. *Relation:* this provides the patching methodology for safety neurons. Our study applies a similar causal approach with language as the contrast, rather than aligned versus unaligned models. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14144)
- **Zhang, Xie, Zhu, Yang, Zhao, Cheng, Wang, Shen, Chua (2026). "Who Transfers Safety? Identifying and Targeting Cross-Lingual Shared Safety Neurons." arXiv:2602.01283.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Finds monolingual safety neurons, then cross-lingual shared ones (SS-Neurons, under 0.3 percent) at the intersection of high-resource and non-high-resource languages. Suppressing SS-Neurons lowers safety across non-high-resource languages, and fine-tuning them improves it. *Relation:* this is the opposite framing to ours. They target *shared* neurons, while we searched for *language-specific* (Hindi) ones. Our finding that Hindi-difference neurons act on English is consistent with safety neurons being shared. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01283)
- **Xie, Shen, Miao, Feng, Yu, Shao, Tian, Du (2026). "Safety Kernel: Mechanistic Alignment for Cross-Lingual Defense in LLMs." arXiv:2610.24614.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Intersects language-wise safety-neuron sets, found with SCAR scores from parallel safe and unsafe prompts, to get a "safety kernel". SK-Masked LoRA lowers attack success, including in held-out languages. *Relation:* another shared-neuron repair method. It supports the claim that the lever is shared rather than per-language. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24614)
- **Miao, Qiu, Shao, Xiao, Shen, Zheng, Chua (2026). "Who Bridges Safety? Identifying and Targeting Cross-Lingual Shared Safety Pathways." arXiv:2608.09095.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Goes beyond single neurons to cross-layer safety pathways. A sparse shared subset bridges high-resource to non-high-resource safety, and tuning only pathway parameters improves non-high-resource safety. *Relation:* this is the circuit-level version of the shared-component view. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09095)
- **Shi, Shen, Shi, Miao, Zhu, Shao, Tang, Chua (2026). "Targeted Interpretable Safety Neuron Enhancement for Multilingual Vision-Language Large Models." arXiv:2604.08881.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Separates FFN safety neurons from general multilingual neurons in VLLMs and applies gradient masking to the safety subspace. *Relation:* peripheral (multimodal), included for completeness. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08881)

### Inferences
- The literature splits into two camps. One claims language-specific safety neurons (SN-Tune). The other, the 2026 Chua/Shen group papers, claims shared safety neurons are the transfer bridge. Our negative Hindi-neuron result supports the shared camp. It also supports Mondal et al.'s broader point that language-specific neurons are not task levers.
- None of these papers reports that intervening on language-associated neurons changes behaviour in the *source* language (English) rather than the target. That aspect of our finding appears novel, but we have not confirmed this exhaustively.

### Gaps
- None of the neuron papers found includes Hindi in safety-neuron experiments (Hindi does appear in some LAPE-style multilingual studies). Their exact language lists were not all checked.
- SN-Tune's arXiv id and its exact multilingual overlap numbers were not verified from the primary text.

## Q2. Latent English pivot (Wendler, Schut, MWork) and implications for safety

### Takeaway
English-dominant models decode intermediate states closest to English in the middle layers. Concepts are largely language-agnostic in the middle, while language identity is encoded early and late. Steering vectors computed in English work better than vectors computed in the input language (Schut et al.). The safety implication is that an English-fitted refusal or harm direction should read non-English inputs in the middle layers *if* the pivot is achieved. Our Qwen/Llama contrast can be read as the pivot succeeding for Hindi harm in Qwen and only partially succeeding in Llama.

### Cited Findings
- **Wendler, Veselovsky, Monea, West (2024). "Do Llamas Work in English? On the Latent Language of Multilingual Transformers." ACL 2024. arXiv:2402.10588.** [V-arXiv; venue from memory, confident] The logit lens on Llama-2 shows three phases. Middle layers decode the semantically correct token but in English. The authors interpret this as an English-biased "concept space" rather than literal English. *Relation:* this is the basis for fitting the refusal direction in English. Our Llama finding (weak Hindi projection) suggests Hindi may not fully reach that concept space for harmful content in Llama-3.1. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10588)
- **Schut, Gal, Farquhar (2025). "Do Multilingual LLMs Think In English?" arXiv:2502.15603.** [V-arXiv; venue UNVERIFIED] Uses the logit lens on French, German, Dutch and Mandarin. Semantically loaded words pass through English-near representations first. Activation steering is more effective with English-computed vectors than with input-language vectors. *Relation:* this directly justifies an English-fitted refusal direction as the measuring stick for Hindi. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15603)
- **Zhao et al. (2024), MWork, NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2402.18815** (see Q1). Middle layers "think in English", and the last layers convert back to the target language. *Relation:* the late language-conversion stage is a plausible site for Qwen's soft wording in Hindi, because refusal intent could survive while the target-language rendering of the refusal weakens. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18815)
- **Dumas, Wendler, Veselovsky, Monea, West (2024/2025). "Separating Tongue from Thought: Activation Patching Reveals Language-Agnostic Concept Representations in Transformers." arXiv:2411.08745** (earlier title "How Do Llamas Process Multilingual Text? A Latent Exploration through Activation Patching"). [V-arXiv; ACL 2025 venue UNVERIFIED] The output language is encoded in the latent at an earlier layer than the concept. Concept and language can be patched independently, and mean cross-lingual concept representations do not hurt translation. *Relation:* this supports separating a harm "concept" from a language component. Our Hindi neurons may act on that language component. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08745)
- **Zhong, Cheng, Liu, Jiang, Wan, Chu, Murawaki, Kurohashi (2024). "Beyond English-Centric LLMs: What Language Do Multilingual Language Models Think in?" arXiv:2408.10811.** [V-arXiv; work in progress at the time] Llama2 uses English as its only latent language. Japanese-continued-pretrained Swallow and balanced LLM-jp show dual English and Japanese latents. *Relation:* the pivot language depends on the training mix. Qwen3, with heavy Chinese and multilingual pretraining, and Llama-3.1 may differ in how Hindi is pivoted, which would be a candidate explanation for our two-mechanism split. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10811)
- **Brinkmann, Wendler, Bartelt, Mueller (2025). "Large Language Models Share Representations of Latent Grammatical Concepts Across Typologically Diverse Languages." arXiv:2501.06346.** [V-arXiv; NAACL 2025 venue UNVERIFIED] SAEs on Llama-3-8B and Aya-23-8B show that grammatical concepts are encoded in shared multilingual feature directions. Ablating them drops classifiers to chance across languages. *Relation:* this is evidence for shared abstract directions, the premise behind testing an English refusal direction on Hindi. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06346)
- **Ferrando, Costa-jussà (2024). "On the Similarity of Circuits across Languages: a Case Study on the Subject-verb Agreement Task." EMNLP 2024 Findings. arXiv:2410.06496.** [V-arXiv] In Gemma 2B, the English and Spanish circuits share an attention head writing a language-independent "subject number" direction. The English-found direction flips Spanish predictions. *Relation:* this is a circuit-level precedent for a single direction controlling behaviour across languages. Our Llama case is a counterexample for Hindi harm, at least in projection magnitude. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06496)
- **Lindsey et al. (Anthropic) (2025). "On the Biology of a Large Language Model." Transformer Circuits Thread.** [UNVERIFIED in session, from memory] Attribution graphs on Claude 3.5 Haiku show language-independent concept features in the middle, with language-specific input and output features. The shared fraction grows with model scale. *Relation:* this supports the "shared middle, language-specific edges" picture. It is not peer-reviewed and covers a closed model. — [Transformer Circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html)

### Inferences
- If the pivot holds, an English-fitted direction should register non-English harm. That is the Qwen case. Llama's weak projection means either the pivot is weaker for Hindi in Llama, or Hindi harm content is routed through a "misaligned subspace". The second is the Minionese framing (Q3).
- The final-layer language-conversion stage gives a mechanistic location for Qwen's soft wording: the decision is intact, but its rendering in Hindi is weaker. This is a hypothesis that none of the papers above tests.

### Gaps
- No pivot-language study found uses Hindi or Devanagari specifically, or Qwen3 specifically.
- Venues for Schut et al., Dumas et al. and Brinkmann et al. were not verified in this session.

## Q3. Mechanistic studies of cross-lingual safety specifically (refusal direction, SAE features, representation transfer)

### Takeaway
The central prior result is Wang et al. (NeurIPS 2025): refusal directions are approximately parallel across 14 languages, so an English direction ablates refusal everywhere. Harmful and harmless prompts separate less well outside English, *especially in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct*. That matches our Llama finding. A cluster of 2026 papers converges on "representation is present, routing or action fails" (Aziz et al.; Kompella and Mahajan; Oppong et al.; Minionese). One paper shows the transfer picture collapses with hard negatives (Balani and Panda). Another shows safety features are entangled with language identity in an architecture-dependent way (Upadhyaya and Sikdar). None reports a two-mechanism split of the "soft refusal versus compliance" kind.

### Cited Findings
- **Arditi, Obeso, Syed, Paleka, Panickssery, Gurnee, Nanda (2024). "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction." NeurIPS 2024. arXiv:2406.11717.** [V-arXiv; venue from memory, confident] Across 13 chat models up to 72B, a single difference-in-means direction mediates refusal. Ablating it removes refusal, and adding it induces refusal. *Relation:* this is our method for the English-fitted refusal direction. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)
- **Wang, Wang, Liu, Schütze, Plank (2025). "Refusal Direction is Universal Across Safety-Aligned Languages." NeurIPS 2025. arXiv:2505.17306.** [V-arXiv; V-web NeurIPS poster page; full text read] Builds PolyRefuse (14 languages, no Hindi) and studies Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and gemma-2-9B-it. Refusal vectors are approximately parallel across languages, so English-derived directions bypass refusal everywhere. The paper reports that "the separation is substantially less distinct in non-English languages, especially in the Llama3.1-8B-Instruct model". Llama's silhouette score falls from 0.496 in English to about 0.22 to 0.32 elsewhere. The authors frame their analysis as downstream of harmfulness identification, citing Zhao et al. 2025. *Relation:* this is the closest prior work. Their Llama separation drop independently anticipates our Llama "weak projection" mechanism. We add Hindi, Qwen3, and the observation that in Qwen the failure is in refusal *wording* rather than in recognition. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17306); [NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/116911)
- **Ivanova (2026). "Is Refusal Direction Universal across Low-Resourced Languages?" BlackboxNLP@EMNLP 2026, Reproducibility Track. arXiv:2610.28897.** [V-arXiv] Extends PolyRefuse to Belarusian, Bashkir and Tajik on Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct. Universality is graded: directions from strongly misaligned languages fail to modulate refusal, and geometric alignment and behavioural transfer are partly dissociated. *Relation:* this supports our finding that projection strength and behaviour can come apart (Qwen: strong projection with soft behaviour). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28897)
- **Aziz, Hanif, Koto (2026). "Low-Resource Safety Failures Are Action Failures, Not Representation Failures." arXiv:2606.01196.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Covers three models and 23 languages. Harmful refusal falls from 87.9 percent in high-resource languages to 43.9 percent in low-resource ones. A harmfulness direction learned from high-resource languages still separates low-resource harmful from harmless prompts, but scores shift downward. A calibrated conditional steering method (low-rank classifier plus add or ablate the direction) reaches a selectivity of 54.5. Llama benefits from target-language calibration, while Qwen and Gemma do not need it. A secondary summary names the models as Qwen, Llama and Gemma-2-9B [model versions UNVERIFIED]. *Relation:* this is the "action failure" thesis. It matches our Qwen story, but their observed downward shift is closer to our Llama story. The Llama-specific calibration need parallels our Llama/Qwen split. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01196)
- **Kompella, Mahajan (2026). "Decided Upstream, Written Late: Locating and Pricing the Cross-Lingual Refusal Circuit of a Multilingual MoE." Actionable Interpretability Workshop, COLM 2026. arXiv:2608.08032.** [V-arXiv] The model is Sarvam, an Indic MoE reasoning model. Harm is encoded as a nearly language-invariant mid-network direction (English versus Indic cosine about 0.9 at layer 11), and steering it controls refusal. But the detection direction is orthogonal to the change that *writes* the refusal, which is assembled late during generation by an MoE "writer" opposed by an attention head. The circuit organisation recurs in a second MoE. *Relation:* this is the closest Indic-language analogue to our Qwen finding: recognition is intact and the failure is in writing the refusal. It differs in architecture (MoE versus dense) and does not contrast with a Llama-type recognition failure. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08032)
- **Oppong, Sahil, Belay, Mukhtar, Abdu, Abdullahi, Oparebea, Aliyu, Abdulmumin, Chilala, Ladislaus, Kondoro, Douglace, Muhammad, Yimam (2026). "The Illusion of Cross-Lingual Safety in Low-Resource Languages." arXiv:2608.11146.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Introduces LoDNA (Twi, Hausa, Amharic, Swahili; literal and localised prompts) and uses a latent geometric refusal probe. Harmful prompts retain less than 10 percent of the English refusal signal in most language-model pairs. The authors conclude that models encode concepts without routing them to safety mechanisms. *Relation:* their "<10 percent of the English refusal signal" result is the extreme form of our Llama finding. Hindi in Qwen is the opposite case. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11146)
- **Ifebi, Kong, Mehrotra (2026). "Minionese: Comprehensive Benchmark and Mechanistic Study of Multilingual LLM Safety." arXiv:2607.10112.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Covers 18 languages, 4 resource tiers, and translation, code-switching, transliteration and translationese perturbations. Low-resource jailbreaks route harm through a "geometrically misaligned subspace" that projects insufficiently onto refusal directions, "leaving the refusal mechanism intact but untriggered". *Relation:* this is the same mechanism as our Llama case. Their transliteration and script-identity result is relevant to Devanagari versus romanised Hindi. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10112)
- **Balani, Panda (2026). "Hard Negatives Reveal What Easy Negatives Hide: Cross-Lingual Harmfulness Representations Degrade with Resource Tier Under Hard Negatives." arXiv:2609.27758.** [V-arXiv; preprint] English-trained harm probes transfer almost perfectly with easy negatives (AUROC above 0.98). With XSTest-style hard negatives, transfer collapses for low-resource languages. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the AUROC drop is 0.003 in English and 0.276 for low-resource languages, and the pattern replicates on Aya Expanse. *Relation:* this is an important methodological caution for our "English-strength recognition" claim in Qwen. It should be checked against hard negatives. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27758)
- **Upadhyaya, Sikdar (2026). "When Safety Speaks a Language: A Mechanistic Analysis of Safety-Language Identity Entanglement in LLMs." arXiv:2608.29936.** [V-arXiv; preprint] Uses SAE features over three instruction-tuned LLMs, eight languages and all layers. Safety features are architecture-dependent in location, geometrically entangled with language identity, and shared to differing degrees. Ablating safety features changes both harmful-response rate *and* output language. A secondary summary says Gemma differs from Llama and Qwen in late-layer sharing [UNVERIFIED]. *Relation:* this is the strongest prior support for two points: (a) safety mechanisms differ by architecture, as in our Qwen/Llama split, and (b) safety and language components are entangled, which may explain why our Hindi neurons have English-side effects. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29936)
- **Li, Yong, Bach (2024). "Preference Tuning For Toxicity Mitigation Generalizes Across Languages." Findings of EMNLP 2024. arXiv:2406.16235.** [V-arXiv] English-only DPO cuts toxicity across 17 languages (mGPT from 46.8 to 3.9 percent). Causal intervention identifies a "dual multilinguality" of MLP layers (multilingual key and value vectors) that explains the transfer. *Relation:* this is a mechanistic account of when English safety training transfers through shared MLP value vectors. Our failure to find Hindi-specific MLP levers is consistent with transfer operating through shared MLP components. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16235)
- **Yeo, Prakash, Neo, Lee, Cambria, Satapathy (2025). "Understanding Refusal in Language Models with Sparse Autoencoders." Findings of EMNLP 2025. arXiv:2505.23556.** [V-arXiv; venue from the ACL Anthology URL 2025.findings-emnlp.338] Finds causally relevant SAE refusal features in two chat models and studies upstream and downstream latents and jailbreak mechanisms. A search summary says harm and refusal are encoded as separate feature sets [UNVERIFIED]. The setting is English only. *Relation:* this is the SAE-level methodology for refusal, and it could be extended to Hindi. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23556); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.338.pdf)
- **O'Brien, Majercak, Fernandes, Edgar, Bullwinkel, Chen, Nori, Carignan, Horvitz, Poursabzi-Sangdeh (2024). "Steering Language Model Refusal with Sparse Autoencoders." arXiv:2411.11296.** [V-arXiv] Amplifying SAE refusal features in Phi-3 Mini improves jailbreak robustness but degrades capability even on safe inputs. *Relation:* this shows that refusal features are entangled with general capability, which parallels our neuron edits having off-target (English) effects. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11296)
- **LessWrong post (n.d., author not recorded). "Low-resourced languages get jailbroken more. Can SAEs explain why?"** [UNVERIFIED: not peer-reviewed; author and date not checked] Reports that English SAE refusal features lose predictive power outside English, and some flip sign (for example in Japanese). — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/8h2KxbPhFx8JoEGdH/low-resourced-languages-get-jailbroken-more-can-saes-explain)
- **Apart Research sprint report (n.d.). "One Direction, Many Languages: Causal Cross-Lingual Refusal Transfer Across Small Open Models."** [UNVERIFIED: hackathon report, not peer-reviewed; authors not recorded] Covers Gemma-2-2B, Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5-1.5B on English, Hindi and Vietnamese. Subtracting the English direction reduces Hindi refusal. A secondary summary notes an internal inconsistency: a Qwen English-Hindi cosine of 0.53 in a table versus 0.85 to 0.97 in the abstract. *Relation:* this is the only other Hindi refusal-direction work found. It uses small models and is of low reliability. — [Apart Research](https://apartresearch.com/sprints/projects/one-direction-many-languages-causal-crosslingual-refusal-transfer-across-small-open-models-teow)
- **Jiang et al. (2026). "One Jailbreak, Many Tongues: Learning Language-Insensitive Intention Representations for Multilingual Jailbreak Detection." arXiv:2606.11202.** [V-arXiv] A detector (MLJailDe) that pulls same-intent jailbreaks together across 11 languages. The work is applied rather than mechanistic. *Relation:* peripheral, a defence-side counterpart. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11202)

Behavioural background, the motivation rather than mechanism:
- **Yong, Menghini, Bach (2023). "Low-Resource Languages Jailbreak GPT-4." NeurIPS 2023 SoLaR Workshop (Best Paper). arXiv:2310.02446.** [V-arXiv] Translating AdvBench into low-resource languages yields 79 percent actionable harmful outputs from GPT-4. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02446)
- **Deng, Zhang, Pan, Bing (2023/2024). "Multilingual Jailbreak Challenges in Large Language Models." ICLR 2024. arXiv:2310.06474.** [V-arXiv] Studies unintentional and intentional multilingual jailbreaks. Unsafe output rises as resource level falls. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06474)
- **Wang, Tu, Chen, Yuan, Huang, Jiao, Lyu (2023/2024). "All Languages Matter: On the Multilingual Safety of Large Language Models." Findings of ACL 2024. arXiv:2310.00905.** [V-arXiv] Introduces the XSafety benchmark (10 languages). All tested LLMs are less safe in non-English. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00905)
- **Shen, Tan, Chen, Chen, Zhang, Xu, Zheng, Koehn, Khashabi (2024). "The Language Barrier: Dissecting Safety Challenges of LLMs in Multilingual Contexts." arXiv:2401.13136.** [V-arXiv; venue UNVERIFIED, possibly Findings of ACL 2024] Lower-resource prompts produce more unsafe *and more irrelevant* responses, and RLHF/SFT on HH-RLHF transfers poorly. *Relation:* the "irrelevant responses" category is the nearest behavioural precedent for degraded refusal *quality* rather than outright compliance. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13136)
- **Yong, Ermis, Fadaee, Bach, Kreutzer (2025). "The State of Multilingual LLM Safety Research: From Measuring the Language Gap to Mitigating It." arXiv:2505.24119.** [V-arXiv; venue UNVERIFIED] A survey of about 300 *ACL papers (2020 to 2024) showing English-centric safety research. It proposes cross-lingual safety generalisation as a direction. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24119)
- **Lemofouet et al. (2026). "LLM Safety Alignment in Low-Resource Languages: A Systematic Literature Review." LM4UC workshop at IJCAI (per arXiv comment). arXiv:2608.14626.** [V-arXiv] A PRISMA review of 50 studies with a taxonomy that includes "mechanistic alignment". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14626)
- **Pattnayak, Chowdhuri (2026). "IndicSafe: A Benchmark for Evaluating Multilingual LLM Safety in South Asia." arXiv:2603.17915.** [V-arXiv] Covers 12 Indic languages and 6,000 prompts. Cross-language agreement is only 12.8 percent. *Relation:* behavioural Hindi and Indic context. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17915)

### Inferences
- Since mid-2026 the field has converged on "recognition intact, action fails" (Aziz et al.; Kompella and Mahajan) and on "weak projection / misaligned subspace" (Oppong et al.; Minionese; Wang et al.'s Llama separation drop). These are the *two* mechanisms our paper finds. Prior papers each argue for one as *the* explanation, whereas we show they co-occur in different model families for the same language.
- The hard-negative caveat (Balani and Panda) is the most important threat to the Qwen "English-strength recognition" claim. That claim should be checked with XSTest-style contrast prompts.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed mechanistic study of Hindi refusal in dense 8B models was found. The only Hindi-specific works are the Sarvam MoE workshop paper and a hackathon report.
- The full texts of most 2026 preprints were not read. Numbers are taken from abstracts.

## Q4. Harmfulness recognition versus refusal execution as separate stages, including multilingual settings

### Takeaway
Zhao et al. (2025) establish that harmfulness (at the last instruction token) and refusal (at post-instruction tokens) are separate directions. Some jailbreaks lower refusal without lowering perceived harm. Qi et al. (2024/ICLR 2025) show refusal behaviour is concentrated in the first few output tokens, which is relevant to how refusals are *worded*. In multilingual settings, Kompella and Mahajan (2026) and Aziz et al. (2026) apply the recognition-versus-execution split directly, and Wang et al. (2025) explicitly position refusal-direction analysis as downstream of harm recognition. No paper found separates a third stage, refusal *wording or strength* given a refusal decision, as our Qwen result does.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhao, Huang, Wu, Bau, Shi (2025). "LLMs Encode Harmfulness and Refusal Separately." arXiv:2507.11878 (v5).** [V-arXiv; venue UNVERIFIED] The harmfulness direction is distinct from the refusal direction. Steering along harmfulness changes the model's judgement, while steering along refusal changes behaviour without changing the judgement. Some jailbreaks suppress refusal while harm belief persists. The authors build a "Latent Guard" from the harmfulness representation. The setting is English. *Relation:* this is the conceptual basis for our Qwen finding (harm recognised, refusal weakened). Note that we measured on a *refusal* direction. Per Zhao et al., recognition should properly be measured on a separate harmfulness direction, so our wording needs care, or we should add a harmfulness-direction check. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11878)
- **Qi, Panda, Lyu, Ma, Roy, Beirami, Mittal, Henderson (2024). "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep." arXiv:2406.05946; ICLR 2025.** [V-arXiv; ICLR venue from memory, confident] Alignment mostly shifts the first few output tokens ("shallow safety alignment"), which explains prefilling, suffix and fine-tuning attacks. *Relation:* if Hindi refusals start with weaker or softer refusal tokens, shallow alignment predicts the rest of the response degrades. This is a plausible mechanism for Qwen's soft wording. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05946)
- **Kompella, Mahajan (2026), arXiv:2608.08032** (see Q3). The harm-detection direction is nearly language-invariant and orthogonal to the refusal-writing change, which happens late during generation. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08032)
- **Aziz, Hanif, Koto (2026), arXiv:2606.01196** (see Q3). Harmfulness is still linearly decodable in low-resource languages, but shifted downward so that it fails to reach the refusal threshold. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01196)
- **Wang et al. (2025), arXiv:2505.17306** (see Q3). They position their analysis at the "refusal extraction layer" as downstream of upstream harmfulness identification, citing Zhao et al. 2025. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17306v2)

### Inferences
- Our Qwen result suggests a three-stage decomposition: recognition, decision, and realisation or wording. The multilingual papers found test only the first two.

### Gaps
- No paper found measures refusal *strength or wording* across languages mechanistically. The behavioural precedent is Shen et al.'s "irrelevant responses", and the multi-turn red-teaming work notes that "mixed" refusals defeat rule-based detectors.

## Q5. Prior work contrasting two model families with different cross-lingual safety mechanisms

### Takeaway
No paper found explicitly argues that two model families fail cross-lingually through *different mechanisms*. Several papers report family-dependent differences in passing. Wang et al. (2025) find Llama-3.1 has the weakest non-English harmful/harmless separation. Aziz et al. (2026) find Llama needs target-language calibration while Qwen and Gemma do not. Upadhyaya and Sikdar (2026) call safety-language entanglement "architecture-dependent". Kompella and Mahajan (2026) find the circuit structure recurs across two MoEs while the lever strength is architecture-specific. Our contribution of a qualitative two-mechanism contrast therefore appears novel, but it is foreshadowed by these observations.

### Cited Findings
- Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct shows "substantially less distinct" non-English harmful/harmless separation than Qwen2.5-7B and Gemma-2-9B, with silhouette scores of 0.496 in English versus about 0.22 to 0.32 elsewhere. — [Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17306v2)
- High-resource-only calibration improves selectivity for Qwen and Gemma, while Llama benefits from target-language calibration. — [Aziz et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01196)
- Safety features are "architecture-dependent" in location and layer distribution, and language-universality of safety is "architecture-dependent". — [Upadhyaya & Sikdar 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29936)
- The refusal-circuit organisation recurs in a second MoE, while "the lever's strength is architecture-specific". — [Kompella & Mahajan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08032)
- Directions from strongly misaligned languages fail to transfer, and geometry and behaviour are partially dissociated (single model, Qwen2.5-14B). — [Ivanova 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28897)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the Llama-specific weak separation (Wang et al.) and the Llama-specific calibration need (Aziz et al.) independently corroborate our Llama "weak projection" mechanism on a different language set. This is worth citing as convergent evidence.
- Prior papers also tested Qwen2.5 rather than Qwen3. Whether Qwen3's English-strength Hindi recognition is a Qwen-family property or a Qwen3 property is untested in the literature.

### Gaps
- No paper found includes both Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B with Hindi in a mechanistic analysis.
- We did not exhaustively check for a 2026 preprint making an explicit "different mechanisms per family" claim. Searches for that framing returned nothing direct.
